Abstract from OpenAlex (for reference; not shown on the site):
Across the United States, outage risk and social vulnerability increasingly coincide, yet few studies have focused on the specific lived experiences and coping mechanisms of marginalized populations. From 2000 to 2021, Michigan ranked second nationwide for large, frequent weather-related power outages. We interview 49 households in Ann Arbor, Michigan's Bryant neighborhood to understand how they recall coping with long-duration outages (≥12 hours), and which energy services residents would prioritize during future long outages if limited backup power were available. By combining semi-structured interviews with household income and utility data (including energy use and cost), we find outage impacts are unevenly distributed, with low-income households, renters, and caregivers facing compounded vulnerabilities. Households adopt a range of adaptive strategies, from short-term coping (e.g., generator use, shifting appliance use) to longer-term preparations, highlighting the role of both individual agency and structural constraints. Our findings illustrate how long outages produce cascading energy insecurity and reveal that resilience is not solely determined by technical capacity but is shaped by intersecting social, economic, and housing vulnerabilities. We conceptualize outage decision-making as a dynamic process influenced by tenure status, caregiving responsibilities, and financial resources, extending existing frameworks of energy vulnerability and resilience. These insights suggest policy interventions and energy system design must account for heterogeneous household capacities and the social dimensions of energy risk, not just infrastructure reliability. By situating the Bryant case within broader energy vulnerability and resilience literature, this study demonstrates how place-based, socially differentiated experiences of outages can inform theoretical understanding and practical strategies for energy justice and equitable energy transitions.
