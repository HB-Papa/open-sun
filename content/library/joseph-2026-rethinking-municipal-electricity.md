Abstract from DOAJ (for reference; not shown on the site):
South Africa’s deepening electricity crisis, marked by persistent load-shedding and escalating tariffs, has accelerated private solar photovoltaic (PV) installations. This rapid transition has raised concerns about municipal revenue erosion, cross-subsidisation and energy inequality. The City of Johannesburg Metropolitan Municipality (CoJ) is at the centre of this transition, and is grappling with the implications for its business model and how private solar PV can be leveraged to support a just energy transition. This paper is written from the perspective of City officials, and examines the extent of rooftop solar PV and its impact on municipal revenue, finances and grid management. The paper draws on municipal records, internal meetings and reports, and an industry dataset. The analysis reveals that official records substantially underrepresent solar PV installations in the CoJ. Despite the concern that private solar PV erodes revenue, this analysis highlights that technical losses, load-shedding, and customer non-payment have a far greater financial impact. This paper challenges the assumption that distributed power undermines social justice, and rather points to the need for tariff reform, away from volumetric sales towards fixed network charges, to stabilise municipal finances. Rather than framing distributed solar PV as inherently threatening to municipal revenue and social justice, the paper shows that these outcomes depend on how municipal business models, tariffs and governance adapt to and leverage off decentralised energy.
