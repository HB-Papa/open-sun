Abstract from OpenAlex (for reference; not shown on the site):
The rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI) is adding a fast-growing workload to the data centres that host it, with consequences for electricity systems, household energy costs, and the environment.This article examines the sustainability implications of AI-driven data-centre expansion in Europe along three connected dimensions-environmental sustainability, energy and social equity, and the sustainability of the digital transition-and asks who gains and who pays.Doctrinal analysis of European Union (EU) law and policy was combined with a comparative, descriptive assessment of published data.The study synthesised evidence from international agencies, EU institutions, national statistical offices, regulators, and peer-reviewed research, and traced household electricity costs and data-centre development from 2023 onward in three purposively selected cases: Ireland, the United Kingdom, and Spain.Data-centre electricity demand, of which AI workloads are an increasingly important component, is growing far faster than overall demand and is highly concentrated geographically.In exposed regions this concentration is associated with grid-capacity constraints and may contribute to upward pressure on network and system costs.Household prices in the period were, however, shaped predominantly by gas prices, taxation, subsidies, renewable output, and network charges, and the study does not estimate causal effects.The expansion also generates environmental externalities, notably carbon emissions and a growing stream of electronic waste.Because energy costs weigh most heavily on low-income households, these pressures carry a distributional risk: a potential "double burden" in which households already in energy poverty benefit least from AI while bearing part of its infrastructure costs.The article maps the EU response by legal status-measures in force, formally proposed, and under discussion-and argues that assessments of AI's social value should internalise its energy, climate, resource, and distributional costs.It closes with actor-specific recommendations on clean-supply additionality, cost-reflective network-cost allocation, safeguards for vulnerable consumers, hardware circularity, and sustainability metrics.
