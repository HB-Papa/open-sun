Abstract from OpenAlex (for reference; not shown on the site):
The expansion of decentralized solar photovoltaic (PV) systems has become central to India's rural electrification and clean energy transition. However, in forest-fringe rural regions, solar adoption often occurs under conditions of livelihood insecurity, limited formal credit access, delayed subsidy support, and continued dependence on forest-based income. This paper conceptualizes the Clean Energy–Forest Degradation Paradox in the Indian context by examining how clean energy adoption may remain embedded within forest-dependent livelihood systems. Unlike generic accounts of renewable energy transition, the study highlights how the Indian manifestation of this paradox is institutionally and economically mediated through Forest Rights Act (FRA)-linked forest governance, non-timber forest product (NTFP)-based cash economies, and subsidy-driven but credit-constrained solar adoption pathways. Using a conceptual and theory-building approach, the paper synthesizes literature on rural solar PV adoption, sustainable livelihoods, forest dependence, energy stacking, and socio-ecological systems. Building on this synthesis, it proposes the Rural Energy–Environment–Livelihood Integration Model (REELIM–India), which integrates three subsystems: energy access, livelihood financing, and ecological–environmental outcomes. The framework proposes that solar PV adoption may reduce kerosene dependence and improve household welfare without necessarily eliminating biomass use, forest-based income dependence, or extractive livelihood practices. Under conditions of weak financing and livelihood vulnerability, forest-derived income may contribute to the purchase, repayment, maintenance, or replacement of solar systems, while continued forest-resource dependence may contribute to localized ecological degradation. The study contributes to energy justice and sustainability-transition literature by integrating technology adoption, livelihood financing, and socio-ecological feedbacks within an India-specific framework. It highlights the need for forest-sensitive clean-energy financing, livelihood diversification, and integrated energy–forest governance to support rural energy transitions that are both socially inclusive and ecologically sustainable.
