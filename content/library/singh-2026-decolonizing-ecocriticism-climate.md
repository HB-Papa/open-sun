Abstract from OpenAlex (for reference; not shown on the site):
Contemporary climate fiction increasingly challenges the assumption that ecological crisis can be understood exclusively through universalized narratives of environmental degradation. Such narratives often obscure the historical inequalities through which ecological harm is produced and unevenly distributed. This article proposes a decolonizing approach to ecocriticism through a comparative reading of Imbolo Mbue’s How Beautiful We Were (2021) and Amitav Ghosh’s Gun Island (2019). While existing criticism has productively examined environmental injustice, climate migration, coloniality, and multispecies relations in these novels, this study brings these concerns together through the intersecting frameworks of postcolonial ecocriticism, environmental justice, slow violence, and more-than-human agency. It argues that both novels challenge anthropocentric and Eurocentric approaches to ecological crisis by locating environmental destruction within histories of extraction, unequal development, displacement, and epistemic hierarchy. Mbue’s representation of Kosawa exposes the relationship between corporate extraction, political power, environmental dispossession, and the struggle for ecological justice. Ghosh, meanwhile, expands the geographical and ontological scale of climate fiction by connecting migration, animals, mythology, colonial history, and planetary transformation. The article further argues that indigenous and local forms of ecological knowledge should not be treated merely as cultural supplements to Western scientific knowledge. Rather, they constitute alternative epistemologies through which human beings may understand their relational responsibilities toward land, animals, and planetary systems. By reading the two novels comparatively, the article develops the concept of decolonial ecological relationality, which describes a literary mode that connects environmental justice with historical memory, epistemic plurality, and more-than-human interdependence. The study concludes that contemporary Anglophone fiction can contribute to ecological thought not simply by representing climate catastrophe but by challenging the colonial assumptions about development, knowledge, ownership, and human exceptionalism that have helped produce the present ecological crisis.
