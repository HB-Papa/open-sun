Abstract from OpenAlex (for reference; not shown on the site):
Purpose: Off-grid solar energy has become an important pathway to electricity access for rural households in Sub-Saharan Africa, yet research has focused largely on adoption and immediate welfare benefits, with less attention to the conditions that sustain use after acquisition. This study examines post-adoption experiences of off-grid solar energy in Tharaka South, Kenya, a semi-arid rural setting with limited access to electricity. It explores how households and local stakeholders understand the benefits of solar energy, the conditions shaping trust in solar products and providers, and the processes through which continued use is supported or constrained. Methodology: The study used the qualitative strand of a convergent parallel mixed-methods design within a pragmatist orientation. Participants were purposively sampled: three focus group discussions with solar-using household heads (youth, women and mixed-gender groups; n = 28) and 22 key informant interviews with administrators, vendors and technicians, community stakeholders and development practitioners. Data were collected in person between December 2025 and March 2026 using semi-structured guides and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. Findings: Five themes were generated: health, safety and educational benefits; uneven economic gains; trust and seller accountability; cost and repair constraints; and community demonstration and peer learning. Households generally valued solar energy, but positive attitudes did not necessarily translate into sustained use. Product failures, inadequate after-sales support, repair difficulties, battery replacement costs and irregular incomes undermined continued use, whereas accountable providers, accessible repair, manageable payment arrangements and visible evidence of successful use strengthened confidence. Unique Contribution to Theory, Practice and Policy: Theory: Diffusion of Innovations, trust in abstract systems and energy justice informed the study as sensitising concepts and were used to interpret themes developed inductively from the data. The study develops conditional acceptance, in which a favourable evaluation of solar is sustained as continued use only when four conditions are met: assured quality, accessible repair, payment fit and visible proof. Unlike post-adoption confirmation, discontinuance or fuel stacking, it locates continued use in the surrounding service ecosystem rather than in the adopter’s evaluation alone. Constrained discontinuance names the outcome when these conditions fail. Practice: providers should ensure genuine products, honoured warranties, local technicians and affordable battery replacement. Policy: policymakers should enforce product standards and vendor registration, support decentralised repair networks, design financing around irregular rural incomes, and monitor sustained functionality rather than installations alone.
