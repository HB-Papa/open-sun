Abstract from OpenAlex (for reference; not shown on the site):
Copper is central to the global energy transition. The world has significant copper reserves, and while there are no concerns about the availability of the metal, the concentration of known deposits within extractive frontiers of the Global South raises persistent questions about its accessibility, and justice issues that arise, and endure, in extractive arenas. Energy justice scholarship has typically approached these questions through a focus on distributive, procedural, and recognition dimensions of justice. This paper extends this work by proposing the concept of justice convergence to theorise a process in which multiple justice dimensions intersect, interact and compound across the life cycle of extractive projects. Drawing on examples of four copper projects in Papua New Guinea (Panguna, Ok Tedi, Wafi-Golpu, and Frieda River) the paper shows that justice convergence operates both synchronously, where different forms of injustice co-exist and co-create at a given moment, and diachronically, where earlier injustices shape and amplify those that follow, producing compound effects over time. Justice convergence is proposed here as a conceptual and practical lens through which scholars, policymakers, and practitioners can better understand and address the enduring inequalities that underpin extractive frontiers, supporting more equitable pathways in mineral development and the energy transition. Based on secondary sources and focused on select case studies, the paper is not intended to establish prevalence of generalisability of justice issues and relations in extractive frontiers. Instead, the case material serves to illustrate how the justice convergence framework can be applied empirically.
