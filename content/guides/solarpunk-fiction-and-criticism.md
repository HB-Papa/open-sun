## Start here

This guide covers two kinds of reading. The first is the fiction itself: stories that imagine hopeful, sun-powered futures. The second is criticism, which here means careful writing about those stories.

If the word is new to you, start with the Field Guide entries for [solarpunk](/field-guide#solarpunk) and [speculative fiction](/field-guide#speculative-fiction).

## Where to search

Most solarpunk fiction is sold in books and magazines, so free and legal stories take some finding. Criticism is easier, because much of it appears in open access journals.

| Tool | What it covers | One tip |
|---|---|---|
| [Center for Science and the Imagination](https://csi.asu.edu/books/) | Free books from Arizona State University, including story collections about solar futures | A good first stop for fiction. |
| [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org) | Free older books that are out of copyright | Try earlier hopeful futures, such as William Morris’s *News from Nowhere*. |
| [OpenAlex](https://openalex.org) | A free, open catalog of research from every field | Turn on the open access filter to hide paywalled results. |
| [DOAJ](https://doaj.org) | Journals and articles that are fully open access | Search for articles, then note which journals keep appearing. |
| [Open Library of Humanities](https://www.openlibhums.org) | A publisher of open access journals in the humanities | Its journals are free to read with no login. |
| [Knowledge Commons](https://hcommons.org) | A repository where humanities scholars share their own work | Useful for conference papers and essays that journals don’t carry. |
| [DOAB](https://www.doabooks.org) | Peer-reviewed open access books | Look for whole books on utopia and climate fiction. |
| [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com) | Research of all kinds, free and paywalled together | Click “Cited by” under a key article to find newer work. |

## Search words that work

Solarpunk is a young word, so searching for it alone is a fine start. Then widen out to the older conversations it belongs to.

| Idea | Words to try |
|---|---|
| The genre itself | solarpunk |
| Stories about climate | "climate fiction", "cli-fi" |
| The wider family | "speculative fiction", "science fiction" |
| Hopeful futures | utopia, utopian, "utopian fiction" |
| Indigenous-led futures | "Indigenous futurism", "Indigenous futurisms" |
| How stories shape energy | "energy imaginary", "energy humanities" |

Put quotation marks around a phrase to search for those exact words together.

```
"climate fiction"
```

In OpenAlex, join ideas with AND and OR in capital letters.

```
solarpunk AND utopia
```

```
"climate fiction" OR "cli-fi"
```

```
solarpunk AND (education OR classroom)
```

In Google Scholar, this finds the word in titles only.

```
allintitle: solarpunk
```

**Search beyond English.** The word “solarpunk” is used unchanged in many languages, so a one-word search also finds work in Spanish, Portuguese and Polish. This library holds readings in all three.

## How to tell if a source is trustworthy

The right questions depend on what you are reading.

- **For fiction,** trust is not about being correct. Ask who published the story, whether an editor chose it, and whether the site has the right to share it. A free copy of a book that is still on sale may not be a legal one.
- **For criticism,** ask whether it appeared in a peer-reviewed journal or book. Peer review means other experts checked the work before it was published.
- **For manifestos, blog essays and forum posts,** remember that they are voices from inside the movement. Cite them as what they are, not as research findings.
- **For everything,** check the date. Solarpunk has changed quickly.

## How to check you can read and reuse it

Look for a license on the story or article. Many open access works use a Creative Commons license, and the guide to [finding open access research](/guides/finding-open-access-research) explains the letters.

Take extra care with fiction and art. A license with “ND” means you may share the work but not a changed version, which includes translations and adaptations. A license with “NC” means you may not use it to make money. If a story shows no license, link to it and don’t copy it.

## Citing what you find

Every item page in this library has a “Cite this” box with APA and Chicago citations you can copy.

When you cite a story from a collection, name the story’s author and title as well as the collection and its editors.
