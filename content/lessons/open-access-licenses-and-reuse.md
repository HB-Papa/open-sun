## Overview

Students who find a free PDF often assume they can do anything with it. This lesson separates two ideas: free to read and free to reuse.

Every item in the Open Sun library is free to read, but the licenses differ widely. Some records say “CC BY 4.0,” others “CC BY-NC-ND 4.0,” and others simply “Free to read.” Information Has Value frames this well. Creators and publishers decide what others may do with their work, and readers who understand those choices can share it with confidence and credit it fairly.

- Make six license cards, each showing one license label as it appears on the item page in this library:
  - CC BY 4.0 ([Hoicka and others](/library/hoicka-2021-reconciliation-renewable-energy))
  - CC BY-SA 4.0 ([A Solarpunk Manifesto](/library/solarpunk-community-solarpunk-manifesto))

## Lesson plan

| Time | Activity | What students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Hands up | Vote yes or no: “If I can read it free, I can post it anywhere.” |
| 5–10 | Who pays? | In pairs, list who might cover the costs of a free article or book, then share. |
| 10–20 | Decode the cards | In pairs, translate a license card into plain words using the guide’s table. |
| 20–28 | Check the source | Find the license on the work itself and compare it with the record. |
| 28–43 | Decision grid | Decide what you may do with three works and write a reason for each choice. |
| 43–50 | Debrief | Compare answers with the key and discuss the hardest case. |

## The activity

1. **Vote.** If you can read something free online, can you post a copy anywhere you like? Keep your answer.
2. **Who pays?** Free to read doesn’t mean free to make. With a partner, list who might pay for editing, hosting and printing.
3. **Decode your card.** Use the license table in [Finding Open Access Research](/guides/finding-open-access-research). Write one sentence for what the license lets you do and one for what it doesn’t.
4. **Check the source.** Open the library record, then click “Read it free.” Look for the license at the top, the foot of the page or the first page of the PDF. Does it match the record?
5. **Spot the difference.** On every Open Sun item page, the license appears after “Open access.” Discuss: does open access always mean you may reuse the work?
6. **Fill in the decision grid.** For each work, answer yes, no or ask permission, and give a reason from the license.

   | Work | Post a full copy on a free class website | Translate a section and share it free | Reprint a section in a zine you sell |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | [Reconciliation through Renewable Energy?](/library/hoicka-2021-reconciliation-renewable-energy), CC BY 4.0 | | | |
   | [Solarities](/library/after-oil-2022-solarities-energy-justice), CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 | | | |
   | [Solarpunk: Notes toward a Manifesto](/library/flynn-2014-solarpunk-notes-manifesto), Free to read | | | |

7. **Add the safe moves.** Write down what you may do with all three, no matter the license.

## Assessment

Decoded cards show the first outcome. The check in step 4 shows the second. The decision grid shows the third.

**Answer key:**

- **CC BY 4.0:** Yes to all three. Credit the authors, link the license and say what you changed, including that a section is translated.
- **CC BY-NC-ND 4.0:** Yes to posting an unchanged copy with credit. No to sharing a translation, which counts as an adaptation. No to the zine, because selling it is commercial. Ask the rights holder, the After Oil Collective, for either.
- **Free to read, no license:** Ask permission for all three. Link to it instead.
- **Safe moves for all three:** Read it, link to it and cite it.

| Outcome | Beginning | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decoding | Reads the letters as a label only | Explains two or three elements correctly | Explains BY, SA, NC and ND correctly in plain words |
| Checking the source | Relies on the record alone | Finds the license but doesn’t compare | Finds the license and notes whether it matches the record |
| Deciding reuse | Answers without reasons | Answers mostly correctly with general reasons | Answers correctly, citing the license element behind each decision |

## Adapting this lesson

- **Online or asynchronous:** Assign one card per student through a form. Share the decision grid as a quiz with a reason box for each answer.
- **Shorter session (30 minutes):** Skip “Who pays?” and decode only three cards.
- **Captions:** Caption any recording and say license abbreviations letter by letter.
- **Screen readers:** Offer the decision grid as a list of nine questions as well as a table. Spell out each license in full on first use.
- **Low vision:** Use large type on cards and don’t rely on color to show yes or no.
- **Students on phones:** The license appears near the top of every Open Sun item page. Licenses on publishers’ pages can sit at the foot, so suggest scrolling to the end.
- **Multilingual learners:** Accept decoded licenses in any language, and invite students to share their own experience of translating.

## Notes for the instructor

- **“It’s free, so it’s public domain.”** No. Free to read only means you can read it. With no license, usual copyright rules apply.
- **“Can I translate an ND work for my own study?”** Creative Commons says the 4.0 NoDerivatives licenses allow adaptations for private use. Sharing them is what’s not allowed.
- **“Can I quote Flynn in my paper?”** Quoting a short passage with a citation is normal academic practice. Creative Commons notes that its licenses don’t limit fair use, which in the United States is judged case by case.
- **“What does SA change?”** If you adapt the manifesto, you must share your version under the same or a compatible license.
- **“Is a class website commercial?”** Creative Commons defines noncommercial as “not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation.” A free course page fits that far better than a zine you sell.
- **The author manuscript card** shows a license can belong to one version. Check the copy you use.
