## Overview

Students often sort sources into “good” and “bad.” This lesson replaces that with a better question: good evidence for what?

Four sources on solar and community energy each come from a different process:

- [Reconciliation through Renewable Energy?](/library/hoicka-2021-reconciliation-renewable-energy), a peer-reviewed journal article
- [Evaluating Community Solar](/library/oshaughnessy-2024-community-solar-equitable-access), a study whose free copy is the authors’ preprint, with a final version in *Nature Energy*
- [Solar Futures Study](/library/doe-nrel-2021-solar-futures-study), a U.S. government report
- [A Solarpunk Manifesto](/library/solarpunk-community-solarpunk-manifesto), a collectively written statement with no date

Authority Is Constructed and Contextual and Information Creation as a Process work well together here. How a source was made shapes what it can tell you.

## Before class

- Open each source and its library record. Confirm that each link still works.
- Share the handouts, especially “How to tell if a source is trustworthy” in [Energy Justice and Community Energy](/guides/energy-justice-and-community-energy) and [Finding Open Access Research](/guides/finding-open-access-research).
- Print or share the source card and the claims grid from the activity.
- Plan groups of four. Each group member takes one source.

## Lesson plan

| Time | Activity | What students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Quick vote | Before reading, vote on which source they would trust most and say why. |
| 5–10 | How sources are made | In pairs, put four labels in order: idea, preprint, peer review, published article. Then discuss where a report and a manifesto fit. |
| 10–28 | Expert groups | Read one source and its record, then fill in a source card. |
| 28–38 | Teach and sort | In mixed groups, teach your source, then match each claim to its best source. |
| 38–47 | Compare | Share matches, settle disagreements and revisit the opening vote. |
| 47–50 | Exit sentence | Write one sentence on what one source is and isn’t good evidence for. |

## The activity

1. **Vote.** Glance at the four titles. Which would you trust most? Write one reason.
2. **Read your source.** Open the library record, then click “Read it free.” Skim the first page, the headings and the end. You don’t need to read every word.
3. **Fill in a source card.**
   - Who made it? A person, a team, a government body or a community?
   - How was it checked before you saw it? Peer review, a preprint server, an organization’s own review, or no stated review?
   - When and where does it apply? Look for dates, places and sample sizes.
   - What is it trying to do? Report findings, model scenarios, or persuade?
   - What is it good evidence for?
   - What is it not good evidence for?
4. **Teach your group.** Take two minutes each to share your card.
5. **Match the claims.** For each claim, choose the strongest source and write one sentence on why.
   - A. In 11 U.S. states, community solar subscribers were more likely to rent than rooftop solar adopters.
   - B. Solar could supply a large share of U.S. electricity by 2035, under certain assumptions.
   - C. Many people in the solarpunk movement want energy that is local and decentralized.
   - D. Indigenous communities in Canada often have little or no ownership of renewable projects on their territories.
6. **Find the limit.** For each source, write one thing it can’t tell you.
7. **Exit sentence.** “[Source] is good evidence for … but not for … because ….”

## Assessment

The source cards show the first and third outcomes. The claims grid and exit sentences show the second.

**Answer key:** A matches the O’Shaughnessy study, B the Solar Futures Study, C the manifesto and D the Hoicka article.

| Outcome | Beginning | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it was made | Calls the source “reliable” or “biased” with no evidence | Names the type, such as “report,” with little evidence | Names the creator and review process, quoting the source or record |
| Matching claims | Matches by topic words alone | Makes correct matches with vague reasons | Makes correct matches tied to how each source was made |
| Limits | Names no limit | Names a limit that applies to every source | Names a specific limit, such as one country, one year, or no review |

## Adapting this lesson

- **Online or asynchronous:** Assign sources at random. Students post cards to a shared board, then complete the claims grid alone and comment on one classmate’s match.
- **Shorter session (30 minutes):** Use only the article, the preprint and the manifesto, and drop the vote.
- **Captions:** Caption any recorded introduction and describe the ordering activity in words.
- **Screen readers:** Give the source card as a plain list, not a form built from images. The Solar Futures Study is a long PDF, so let students start from its record summary and then skim the headings.
- **Low vision:** Allow zoom and offer large-print cards.
- **Students on phones:** The library records and the manifesto are short web pages. The Solar Futures Study is a long PDF, so give it to someone on a laptop.
- **Multilingual learners:** Offer sentence starters for the card and accept answers in any language. The manifesto is the shortest text.

## Notes for the instructor

- **“Peer-reviewed means true.”** It means other experts checked the methods before publication. Hoicka’s article is strong evidence about 194 projects in Canada, not about every country.
- **“So the preprint is weak?”** Not necessarily. This study was later published in *Nature Energy*. The free copy came first, so its wording or numbers may differ. Students should use the “Publisher’s page (DOI)” link to check the final version before quoting.
- **“A government report is neutral.”** The Solar Futures Study models what could happen if costs fall and policies support solar. It is good evidence for an official scenario, not a forecast.
- **“A manifesto isn’t evidence.”** It is excellent evidence of what a movement says it values. It isn’t evidence that those ideas work. The manifesto credits its own sources, including Adam Flynn’s essay, which shows how movement texts build on each other.
- **Disagreement is useful.** If groups split on a match, ask what kind of evidence would settle it.
